Why it matters

A continent of languages — missing from the machines

Roughly 2,000 of the world's languages — close to a third of them — are African. Yet our languages barely register in the technology the world now runs on.

Colonialism pushed African languages out of schools, offices and print, and that neglect followed them into the digital age. The result is technology that stumbles over our names, misreads our cultures, mislabels our places and forgets our history. Voice assistants do not hear a Gĩkũyũ grandmother; translation tools flatten Sheng; maps misspell the villages on our own ridges.

PBAG grew up telling stories in the languages people actually speak — Ngaahika Ndeenda was performed in Gĩkũyũ at Kamĩrĩĩthũ for exactly that reason. Research is how we carry that work into technology.

Our goal

We want Africans to shape — and own — the language technologies that are coming, and to steer them towards human dignity, well-being and fairness. We get there by building community together, by doing research in the open with the people it serves, and by bringing artists, linguists, technologists and elders to the same table.

How we work

Data archaeology

Good language technology starts with good data — and for African languages, much of it is buried in archives, memories and performances. We dig it up carefully, and we create new data with communities rather than about them.

{{i:search}}

Excavating the record

Finding and digitising Gĩkũyũ and Kiswahili text in colonial files, church and school publications, newspapers and family papers — the same digging behind Ithaka cia Kamĩrĩĩthũ.

{{i:mic-vocal}}

Voices from the stage

With performers' consent, turning scripts, rehearsals and live shows into speech and text — natural, expressive language that reading corpora miss.

{{i:users}}

Inclusive gathering

Story circles, elders' sessions and youth Sheng workshops, designed so that women, rural speakers, elders and young people are all heard — so the data truly represents the culture.

{{i:map-pin}}

Names & places

A community gazetteer of Kiambu place names, clans and landmarks, linked to the Heritage Map — so technology spells and says our places right.

{{i:wand-sparkles}}

Testing the models

Evaluating how today's AI translation, speech and chat models handle Gĩkũyũ, Kiswahili and Sheng — and publishing where they fail, so they can improve.

{{i:graduation-cap}}

Skills & ownership

Training young Kenyans in data collection, annotation and AI so the expertise — and the value — stays in the community.
