Prologue

A flat place where the water never dries

Ithaka is land — not land as a title deed, but land as lineage: the soil your grandmother cleared, the ridge your clan defended, the grave that tells you who you are. Kamĩrĩĩthũ takes its name from mirithu, a flat place on which rests a pool of water defiant to drought. It sits on the cool roof of Limuru, about 2,500 metres up on the eastern lip of the Great Rift Valley, between the Narok road, the Limuru–Mutarakwa road and today's Nairobi–Nakuru highway.

Every ridge here has two histories: the one written in colonial files, and the one carried in songs, names and graves. This story tries to hold both — and it is the soil from which PBAG Theatre's new production grows.

Chapter I · Oral tradition

In the beginning: Gĩkũyũ, Mũmbi and the nine daughters

The Agĩkũyũ tell that Ngai — the Divider, the Giver — took Gĩkũyũ to the summit of Kĩrĩnyaga (Mount Kenya) and showed him the land below: ridges, rivers and forest. At its heart stood a grove of fig trees, and there, at Mũkũrwe wa Nyagathanga in today's Murang'a, Gĩkũyũ was told to build his home. Ngai gave him a wife, Mũmbi — "the one who moulds, who creates".

They had daughters — nine of them by the most common telling — and from these daughters came the clans (mĩhĩrĩga) that still organise kinship among the Agĩkũyũ. A tenth, Wamũyũ (or Warigia), appears in some versions.

Chapter II · Before the maps

Ridges, forests and the stone of fear

Long before fences, the southern Kikuyu frontier was moving land. Families pushed into the forests of what became Kiambu, clearing ground and founding mbari — lineage estates whose boundaries were remembered by elders rather than surveyors. To the north-west, the grassy country the Maasai called Lari, "the place of green grass", served as dry-season grazing.

The landscape held its own institutions. In Ndeiya, below Limuru, a rock leans over a cliff: Ihiga rĩa Gũoya, the Stone of Fear. Local tradition remembers it as a place where boys became men; nearby stands Ihiga rĩa Nyondo. Today hikers climb there for the view across the plains — few know they are walking an initiation path.

Chapter III · 1890 – 1930

Strangers on the ridge

  1. 1890Captain Frederick Lugard of the Imperial British East Africa Company camps on the land of Waiyaki wa Hinga near Dagoretti and builds a fort.
  2. 1891Company porters loot farms; Waiyaki's warriors raze the garrison. Kinyanjui wa Gathirimu helps the company men withdraw — and is later rewarded.
  3. Aug 1892At "peace talks" at Fort Smith, Waiyaki is wounded and arrested near today's Kabete Police Station. He dies on the road to the coast, at Kibwezi.
  4. c. 1900The Uganda Railway climbs past Limuru toward the Rift. Settlers follow — drawn by fertile soil, cool weather and a short ride to Nairobi.
  5. 1903Tea is planted at Limuru — the beginning of Kenya's tea industry. The ridge becomes part of the "White Highlands".
  6. 1930St Paul's Divinity School moves to Limuru. Mission, estate and railway now frame Kikuyu life on the ridge.
Chapter IV · Dossier

The chiefs: resisters, allies and those caught between

The British ruled through appointed chiefs. Some were elders with real standing; others were the Company's men. Their choices — to resist, to accommodate, to collaborate, or to petition from inside — shaped who kept their land. History rarely sorts them neatly; this dossier sets out what the records say.

Resisted

Waiyaki wa Hinga

Dagoretti · d. 1892

Mũthamaki of southern Kikuyu country. Treated with Lugard, then fought the Company when its garrison abused his people. Arrested at Fort Smith in 1892 and killed at Kibwezi. Waiyaki Way in Nairobi bears his name.

Allied with the British

Kinyanjui wa Gathirimu

Dagoretti · Kabete · c. 1865–1929

Company porter and interpreter who became its close ally — led punitive raids, sold southern Kiambu land to early settlers from 1902, and was made the only Kikuyu paramount chief. Died at Thogoto in 1929.

Petitioned · later detained

Koinange wa Mbiyu

Kiambaa · c. 1865–1960

Senior Chief of Kiambu from 1938. Opposed land alienation and campaigned for Africans' right to grow coffee. Detained in 1952 at about 87; released on 1 July 1960, he died 19 days later.

Loyalist chief

Waruhiu wa Kung'u

Ruiru · Githunguri · d. 1952

Senior chief who publicly condemned Mau Mau. Shot near Gachie on 7 October 1952; within two weeks the colony was under a State of Emergency.

Between two fires

Luka wa Kahangara

Kirenga, Lari · d. 1953

Removed from Tigoni with his people, he had worked as a settler's farm supervisor before becoming chief at Kirenga. Killed with members of his family on the night of 26 March 1953 — the Lari Massacre.

Loyalist chiefs

Ikenya Charles & Makimei wa Kuria

Bathi · Gituamba (Lari)

Also targeted on 26 March 1953. Both escaped; their property was burned.

Reformers

Josiah Njonjo & Njiiri wa Karanja

Kikuyu Association · 1919

With Koinange and Waruhiu, founded the Kikuyu Association to carry grievances to the colonial government through official channels.

Kamĩrĩĩthũ

Chief Kamiri & Chief Jairus

Kamĩrĩĩthũ

Named in local records as chiefs associated with Kamĩrĩĩthũ. Their full stories are part of PBAG's ongoing oral-history gathering.

Colonial chief · contested rule

Ex-Chief Mũkoma wa Njiriiri

Kiambu / Limuru · colonial era

A prominent colonial-era chief of the Kiambu–Limuru area, remembered in full as Ex-Chief Mũkoma wa Njiriiri. He served as a chief under British rule in the Kiambu/Limuru region.

A contested tenure. Robert L. Tignor's The Colonial Transformation of Kenya: The Kamba, Kikuyu, and Maasai from 1900 to 1939 (Princeton, 1976) notes that the colonial government recorded heavy emigration from Chief Mũkoma's location, attributing it directly to the "petty oppression" that existed under his rule — a reminder that the chief system often pushed families off their land as surely as settlers did.

His life in print. His history, life and times are explored in the biographical book Ex-Chief Mũkoma wa Njiriiri by Mbũrũ wa Njiriiri.

Context: the seer Mugo wa Kibiru foretold the coming of the strangers and the "iron snake" railway; tradition holds he prophesied that a great mũgumo at Thika would wither at independence.

Chapter V · 1939

From Tigoni to Lari: the wound in the land

In the 1930s the colonial Land Commission weighed Kikuyu claims to the ridges around Limuru. In 1939 families were moved off Tigoni so that settlers could plant coffee and tea: members of ten mbari and their tenants were relocated — Tigoni people to Kimende, Githirioni and Kirenga in Lari, Kiambu people to Bathi. At first they were not compensated.

“The Lari Massacre had its origins in the early 1900s.”— J. G. Kiereini, A Daunting Journey, as quoted in Business Daily

Among those moved was a man named Luka, who worked as a supervisor on a settler farm and later became chief of Kirenga. In Lari, the question of who had been given land, and who had lost it, set neighbour against neighbour. When the Emergency came, settlers in Tigoni formed their own militia — the "Tigoni Tigers", an offshoot of the Limuru Farmers Association.

Chapter VI · 1952 – 1957

Fire on the ridge

On 20 October 1952 the Governor declared a State of Emergency. The Kenya Land and Freedom Army — Mau Mau — took to the forests: Kereita, Kinale, Ragia and on into the Aberdares. Caves in Kereita and Ragia still mark where fighters sheltered.

On the night of 26 March 1953, fighters attacked loyalist homesteads at Lari. Huts were set alight; families were cut down as they fled. Chief Luka and thirteen members of his family died. Historians commonly cite about 74 dead; other accounts give higher figures. The colonial government showed the scene to journalists to make its case to the world — and its forces and the Home Guard then went door to door. Reprisals killed about 400 people by some estimates, and survivors' accounts suggest far more. Of 309 people prosecuted, 136 were convicted and 71 hanged. Many of the dead lie in a mass grave in Kereita Forest.

Lari shows the Emergency as it was lived: not only settlers against Africans, but a civil war among neighbours over land, loyalty and fear.

In 1955 Kamĩrĩĩthũ itself was burned and its people herded into an Emergency village behind trenches and guard posts — the village that became today's Kamĩrĩĩthũ. In one Kamĩrĩĩthũ household a half-brother, Mwangi, joined the forest fighters and was killed; another brother was shot; the mother was tortured at the Kamĩrĩĩthũ Home Guard post. A schoolboy in that household would one day write it all down.

Chapter VII · 1938 – 2025

The son of Kamĩrĩĩthũ

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o was born in Kamĩrĩĩthũ on 5 January 1938. He left Limuru for Alliance High School in 1955, in the middle of the Emergency, and went on to Makerere and Leeds. His novels — Weep Not, Child (1964), The River Between (1965), A Grain of Wheat (1967), Petals of Blood (1977) — carried the ridges of Limuru to the world.

Then he came home. In 1976 villagers founded the Kamĩrĩĩthũ Community Education and Cultural Centre. With Ngũgĩ wa Mĩriĩ they wrote Ngaahika Ndeenda (I Will Marry When I Want), in Gĩkũyũ, performed by workers and peasants in an open-air theatre they built themselves. It opened on 2 October 1977 before more than 10,000 people.

  1. Nov 1977The centre's public-performance licence is withdrawn.
  2. 31 Dec 1977Ngũgĩ is arrested and held without trial at Kamiti Maximum Security Prison, where he writes Caitaani Mũtharaba-inĩ (Devil on the Cross) in Gĩkũyũ.
  3. Dec 1978Released; he will later go into exile.
  4. 11 Mar 1982All theatre activity at Kamĩrĩĩthũ is banned while Maitũ Njugĩra (Mother, Sing for Me) is in rehearsal.
  5. 12 Mar 1982Three truckloads of armed police raze the open-air theatre.
  6. 28 May 2025Ngũgĩ dies in Buford, Georgia, USA, aged 87.

A village stage was demolished because people were telling their own story, in their own language, about their own land. That is the inheritance PBAG — born in Limuru in 2011 — carries onto the stage.

Survey · physical features

The land itself: ridges, forests, waters and sacred trees

Limuru sits on the cool, rain-fed roof of Kiambu, about 2,500 metres up, where the highlands break suddenly into the Great Rift Valley. Its soils grow tea and dairy; its forests fed the Mau Mau; its swamps feed Nairobi's rivers.
